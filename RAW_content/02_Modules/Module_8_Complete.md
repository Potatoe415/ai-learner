## **Welcome to Module 8: Ethics and Capstone**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303699/preview)

Digital transformation is no longer only about adopting new technologies — it is about making responsible choices in how those technologies shape organizations and society. With the rapid rise of generative and agentic AI, leaders must confront a new set of ethical questions alongside technical and strategic ones. These systems can generate content, make decisions, and act autonomously at scale, but they can also introduce bias, reduce transparency, and amplify risk. As AI becomes more deeply embedded in workflows, ethics is not a peripheral concern; it is central to ensuring fairness, accountability, and trust across employees, customers, and stakeholders.

In this module, we make the ethical dimension explicit while bringing the course together through the Capstone. You will examine core principles, such as fairness, transparency, accountability, and data stewardship, and consider how they apply to real-world AI deployments. At the same time, you will consolidate insights from across the course to develop a structured, actionable AI adoption plan. By the end of this module, you will be prepared not only to design AI-driven solutions but also to evaluate and implement them responsibly, balancing performance with purpose.

## **1. Ethics in AI**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303963/preview)

### **Ethics**

### **Why Ethics Comes Last**

We have placed ethics at the end of the course intentionally;   it is not an afterthought but the integrative lens through which all prior decisions must be evaluated.   You have learned about AI strategy, governance, risk, compliance, and technology. Ethics asks the deeper questions: Just because we can apply AI, should we? Under what terms? How do we taking into account human, societal, and stakeholder implications?

This orientation connects directly to your capstone. Your final project must not only demonstrate technical acuity and strategic insight, but also ethical judgment. You will need to consider how your proposed AI interventions impact employees, customers, regulators, and society. Ethics, in effect, becomes the final test of responsible leadership.

### **Why AI Ethics Is Different From Traditional Tech**

AI differs from traditional technologies in three key ways: It is often opaque, operates probabilistically, and scales decisions rapidly, amplifying both value and risk.

Moreover, AI works at scale and speed, so errors are amplified. A flawed formula in a spreadsheet might affect one team; a flawed AI model can impact thousands or millions. This introduces new urgency for ethical foresight, making ethics not a barrier, but a strategic imperative.

### **Core Ethical Principles for AI in Business**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303962/preview)

Several widely cited principles have   emerged   as guideposts from both practice and scholarship. Four   of them   stand   out for leaders:

*Select each tab to learn more.*

**Fairness and non-discrimination**

AI can replicate or entrench bias if trained on skewed data. Business leaders must ensure systems do not disadvantage individuals based on protected characteristics. Transparency and fairness are both ethical and profitable, making AI fair can improve organizational performance (Satell & Abdel‑Magied, 2020; Kaushik & Kai, 2024).

**Transparency and explainability**

Black‑box systems breed distrust. Leaders need systems whose decisions can be meaningfully communicated to employees, customers, and regulators in plain English.

**Accountability and responsibility**

Autonomy does not absolve responsibility. Leaders must retain oversight and ethical accountability for outcomes attributed to AI.

**Privacy and data stewardship**

With increasing data regulation, mismanagement is both a reputational and legal risk. Responsible leaders treat data with care and purpose.

### **Business Risks of Ethical Failures**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7304051/preview)

When AI fails ethically, business fallout can be swift and severe.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

**Biased hiring algorithms**

In 2018, Amazon scrapped an AI recruitment tool after it was discovered to be systematically downgrading résumés that included the word “women’s,” such as “women’s chess club captain.” The model had been trained on historical data that reflected a male-dominated tech industry, and so it learned to favor men over women in hiring recommendations. The case became a cautionary tale, showing how unexamined training data can encode and amplify workplace discrimination (Dastin, 2018). For organizations, the reputational damage was significant, as media outlets portrayed the incident as evidence of bias “baked into” AI. It also underscored legal risks, since discriminatory practices can trigger lawsuits or regulatory action under equal employment laws.

**Deepfakes and disinformation**

AI-generated deepfakes are already being weaponized for fraud and disinformation. In one high-profile case, criminals used voice-cloning software to impersonate an organization’s director and trick a UK energy organization into transferring $243,000 to a fraudulent account (Damianai, 2019). Beyond financial scams, deepfakes are also spreading into politics: In 2024, the U.S. Federal Communications Commission banned AI-generated robocalls after a fake voice message imitating President Biden attempted to mislead voters (Swenson, 2024). For organizations, this threat goes beyond reputational damage — it can undermine trust in executives, advertising, and even customer communications.

**Healthcare misdiagnosis**

AI systems are increasingly used in medicine to support cancer screening, stroke detection, and diagnosis of other serious conditions. While these tools can improve accuracy, errors raise pressing legal and ethical questions: If an AI tool misses a diagnosis and a patient is harmed, who is responsible: the doctor, the developer, or the hospital? Current regulations provide little clarity, leaving liability blurred across providers and AI vendors. Experts argue that without clear policies, patients may be left unprotected when AI errors occur, even as adoption accelerates (Bose, 2025).

Ethical AI missteps can lead to loss of trust, disengaged staff, customer backlash, and regulatory scrutiny.

In this respect, ethical failures are not just moral missteps but are business hazards. Integrating ethics into governance is not philanthropic; it is risk management.

### **Definition: Model Card**

A model card is a standardized document that outlines an AI model’s purpose, data sources, performance, limitations, and risks.   The concept, introduced by Google researchers in 2019, helps organizations evaluate whether an AI system is appropriate, fair, and safe for a given business application (Desai, 2023).

### **Ethics and Corporate Leadership**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303957/preview)

Leaders set the tone. Ethical AI can become a brand differentiator when rooted in strategic clarity.

Examples: Leaders in AI ethics such as the Partnership on AI convene diverse stakeholders to set norms and guidance on responsible deployment. Leaders foster trust when they embed ethics into governance, not just in mission statements, but in operations, training, and accountability structures.

Ethical leadership balances performance with purpose. The question shifts from "How far can we push efficiency?" to "How can we advance responsibly and sustainably?" (Moltazu, 2019).

### **Global and Cultural Perspectives**

Ethics is not universally uniform:

* Europe often leads privacy-first norms.
* The United States favors innovation-first approaches with emerging oversight.
* Asia varies: some countries enact state-led experimentation and regulation.

Global leaders must respect legal and cultural differences while upholding core principles like fairness, accountability, and human welfare.

**The Capstone**

**Brief description:** This assignment is **mandatory** and **will be evaluated** as part of your final grade. This assignment must be completed **individually**. Before attempting the assignment, you should study the module content.

### **Introduction**

We have reached the end of the course. Your final project will consolidate the knowledge, skills, and frameworks you have developed while tailoring them to the specific context of your organization.

Throughout the course, you have completed practical exercises with AI tools and examined critical considerations such as cost, security, employee adoption, change management, and KPIs. This final assignment builds on that work.

Rather than creating entirely new material, your task is to bring together your previous ideas and apply them in a coherent, actionable plan for AI adoption.

### **Instructions**

You will create a two- to five-page document outlining how you would integrate AI into a function or process within your organization. Please include the following:

* **Context**
  + Identify your sector and type of organization.
  + Specify the department, process, or function where AI will be implemented.
* **Technology choice**
  + State which AI system(s) you will use, and why.
* **Cost considerations**
  + Provide an estimated cost, including capital expenditure, operational expenses, and any staffing or training costs.
* **Security plan**
  + Outline how you will address data privacy, cybersecurity, and risk mitigation.
* **Change management and training**
  + Describe your plan for employee adoption, communication, and training.
* **Scaling strategy**
  + Explain how you will roll out the implementation, using the “crawl, walk, run” maturity model.
* **Governance and compliance**
  + Describe how you will ensure compliance with relevant regulations and governance standards.
* **KPIs**
  + Identify the measurable indicators you will use to evaluate the success of your AI implementation.

**Format:** Word/PDF document

**Submit your work:** To upload your work, click on "Start assignment" at the top right corner of the page. You must attach the document, then click on "Submit assignment" again.

The average time to complete this assignment is 120 minutes.

**Module 8: Wrap-Up**

---

As you have seen throughout this module, the successful adoption of AI is not only a technical or strategic challenge, but an ethical one. As organizations integrate increasingly powerful generative and agentic systems into their workflows, questions of fairness, transparency, accountability, and human impact become central to long-term success. By engaging with these ethical principles and examining real-world risks, you are now better equipped to evaluate AI initiatives not only for what they can achieve but also for how they should be implemented.

At the same time, this module has brought together the full scope of the course, connecting each concept, framework, and activity to the development of your Capstone project. You now have the tools to design a structured, actionable AI adoption plan — one that balances innovation with responsibility, performance with purpose, and ambition with sound governance.

**Captain’s log** 

**We want to hear your voice!** 

We are inviting you to record a **30 to 60 second video** where you will share your personal and professional experience. You can include your thoughts about learning, process, doubts that came up, your perspectives, describe your breakthrough moment, and summarize what this module has meant for you. 

Your captain’s log is **completely yours**and will only be accessible to your learning facilitator, who will use it to evaluate your understanding of the course content. 

You can also share your **opinion about the program**, but don’t just stick to the indications, we encourage you to express your opinions and perspectives on the topics covered. Look at this activity as an opportunity to reflect, self-assess and make the most of your learning! 

For more information, see the [Captain's Log page](/courses/17497/pages/captains-log "Captains Log").

**Assignment submission format:** Review the [Options for Video Upload document](/courses/17497/files/7303666?wrap=1) to see how to share a video via an external link.

*This activity is private, so only the facilitator will have access to your video. If you do not wish to share your personal image, you can submit a presentation or record a voice message.* 

The video must have a maximum duration of one minute.

# Module 8: Q&A Discussion Board

*Original URL: https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/discussion_topics/1120978?module_item_id=3633541*

![](/courses/17497/files/7303700/preview)

Use this Q&A discussion board to share your insights and post your questions as you engage with the material from this module. Your Learning Facilitator will moderate this forum and respond to your content-related questions, typically within 48 hours. This discussion board may also assist you and your Learning Facilitator in preparation for office hours or live sessions.

You are not required to participate in this discussion, and there is no grade associated with it.

To make this most useful for all participants:

* **Bookmark** this page and return to it often during this module.
* **Search** for your topic first (use the search box below).
* If someone has asked a similar question, **respond to that particular thread**to keep the conversation contained. This will allow your fellow participants to spot specific topics more easily and offer their insights.
* Create a **new thread** if your question has not been addressed.
* Start your post with a brief **subject statement** that specifies the topic or problem.
  + Examples:
    - Assignment 8.1, Question 3
    - Installing Miniconda issue
    - Sharing an interesting article about X
* **Post questions and topics related to this module’s** content and be as specific as possible when referring to program content or learning activities.
  + Example: In Video 8.2 at 4.10
* **Reply**! Share your thoughts and insights.
