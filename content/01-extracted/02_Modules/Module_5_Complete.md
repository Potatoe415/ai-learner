## **Welcome to Module 5: AI Agents by Business Function**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303687/preview)

Many organizations begin their AI journey with isolated experiments—testing chatbots, summarizing documents, or automating customer replies. But the real value of agentic AI comes when these tools move beyond novelty and become embedded in core business functions.

This unit explores how organizations can scale AI from pilot projects to enterprise-wide transformation. It begins with the AI Maturity Cycle—a five-stage framework that guides companies from identifying use cases to fully deploying agentic systems across workflows. But maturity is only the starting point.

From there, we examine how agentic AI reshapes product development cycles, enabling faster prototyping, autonomous testing, and design iteration with minimal human input.

In this module, we shift our focus to health care, one of the most complex and high-stakes industries where agentic AI is already making a measurable difference. You will explore how intelligent agents are being applied in clinical decision support, medical imaging, personalized care, and hospital operations, and what these applications mean for both patient outcomes and organizational efficiency.

We also explore how AI is disrupting business process outsourcing (BPO), offering scalable, autonomous solutions that reduce cost and expand capacity without increasing headcount.

Whether the goal is to accelerate product innovation, reimagine internal operations, or automate customer-facing support, the pathway is similar: start small, build foundational knowledge, and scale with structure and intention.

Across each section, you will see how organizations move from experimentation to repeatable performance—and ultimately, to a sustainable AI-driven advantage.

**Stage 1: Identify Opportunities**

Organizations initiate their AI journey by pinpointing tasks that are well-suited for **automation** or **augmentation.** Such tasks often involve repetitive processes or recurring bottlenecks.

For instance, a sales team may spend significant time conducting account research. By breaking this work into discrete actions (or workflows, as we prefer to call them), such as selecting target companies, enriching profile data, and drafting outreach messages, the team might be able to develop an AI agent that can generate a draft report within minutes rather than hours. Similarly, software engineers might employ agentic code editors to develop functional prototypes in a single workday. By requesting and refining code snippets in stages, they accelerate development cycles and reduce manual iteration.

The most important consideration at this stage is securing stakeholder buy-in. This will involve demonstrating tangible benefits and building user familiarity with AI capabilities.

**Stage 2: Teach AI Fundamentals**

After identifying high-value opportunities, organizations must establish a common foundation of knowledge. In the process of cultivating AI literacy, stakeholders learn how to craft effective prompts, interpret model outputs, and anticipate typical limitations.

**Governance**

Governance policies should be defined to mitigate risks such as Shadow AI, which occurs when employees use unsanctioned AI tools without authorization. Clear policies and oversight help ensure that AI use aligns with organizational standards and compliance requirements.

**Tools**

To ensure consistent tool use, organizations may roll out approved large language model editors, agent platforms, and conversational analytics interfaces. Standardizing tools helps teams work efficiently while reducing fragmentation and risk.

**Ethics**

Ethical guardrails guide teams by establishing standards for fairness, transparency, data privacy, and accountability. Together, these measures ensure that early AI experiments proceed safely, responsibly, and consistently.

**Stage 3: Map Workflows**

Workflow mapping involves analyzing existing processes to identify where AI can be most effective. To get started, teams might reimagine their daily tasks with agentic AI support.

Dr. Sanchez uses LinkedIn account management as an illustration of workflow mapping (as seen in the diagram below) as well as a potential use case. An HR team might split the task of LinkedIn account management into distinct workflows, such as connection outreach, profile enrichment, message personalization, and engagement tracking. By mapping these components, it will help the team to envision how specialized AI agents might automate some of these tasks, thereby improving efficiency.

Once workflow maps have been generated, prototypes might be developed and scored based on effort, value, and risk. Based on these evaluations, the most promising initiatives could then be selected for further development.

**Stage 4: Provide Enablement, Resources and Share Success**

Once pilot programs have been selected, the organization must allocate resources to move solutions toward production readiness. Cross-functional development teams work to integrate AI agents into secure enterprise platforms, while robust data pipelines and standardized API interfaces ensure reliable data flow. Performance dashboards are used to measure improvements in time savings, error rates, and throughput.

For example, a customer service organization might deploy an AI agent to assist with support tickets. The agent is integrated into the organization’s CRM system, connected to its knowledge bases through APIs, and supported by a data pipeline that continuously updates product information. A dashboard then tracks metrics such as average resolution time and customer satisfaction, allowing teams to evaluate performance, refine the system, and identify high-ROI solutions for broader adoption.  
   
As AI-driven processes mature, they are incorporated into standard operating procedures. Organizations publish detailed case studies that describe agent architecture, development methodology, and governance protocols. They report clear performance metrics, such as reductions in handling time and improvements in review speed.

For example, a financial services firm might report that its AI-powered document review agent reduced contract review time by 40% while maintaining compliance accuracy, enabling legal teams to process significantly higher volumes without increasing headcount.

During this stage, executive sponsorship and coaching are crucial to support teams through the navigation of technical and organizational challenges. Communities of practice and AI champions emerge to share best practices and foster continuous innovation. Embedding AI capabilities in this manner creates a virtuous cycle of adoption and organizational transformation.

### **Keep in Mind**

#### **Shadow AI**

The democratization of AI has had major benefits as well as drawbacks. While the wider dissemination of user-friendly AI tools means that employees can now access advanced AI capabilities far more easily, it also allows them to do so without the knowledge or permission of their IT and security teams (IBM, 2024). The unauthorized use of AI tools can expose companies to risks such as data leakage, security vulnerabilities, noncompliance with regulations (e.g., the General Data Protection Regulation), and reputational damage.

Shadow AI manifests in many ways. Common examples include AI chatbots, external machine learning models, and AI-powered data visualization tools. A survey of 7,000 individuals across the U.S., U.K., Canada, Germany, Australia, India, and New Zealand, conducted jointly by CybSafe and the National Security Alliance, found the  **38%**  of employees admitted having shared sensitive data in the workplace through the use of such tools without permission. This rose to  **46%**  and  **43%**  among the Gen Z and Millennial cohorts, respectively (National Cybersecurity Alliance & CybSafe, 2024).

### **Mitigating Risks of Shadow AI?**

**Policy**    Building on the understanding that democratized AI tools empower employees while introducing risks of unauthorized use, organizations must take a comprehensive, proactive approach. They should establish clear governance frameworks that incorporate sanctioned AI tools aligned with security and compliance standards, along with detailed AI use policies. Regular audits of AI tool usage and the formation of cross-functional AI governance teams or “Centers of Excellence” provide continued oversight and ensure alignment with enterprise risk policies.

**Technology**    From a technical perspective, organizations must implement advanced monitoring and control measures. These include data loss prevention tools, network traffic filtering, and role-based access controls that detect and block unauthorized AI activities. Reviewing input and output logs can expose covert Shadow AI practices, while secure environments for AI experimentation help protect sensitive data. Providing easy access to approved, enterprise-grade AI solutions also reduces the likelihood that employees will seek unsanctioned alternatives.

**Culture**    In addition to policy and technology, organizations must foster a culture of transparency and responsible AI use. Continuous employee education ensures that teams understand both the benefits and risks of AI tools. Encouraging safe experimentation within approved environments promotes innovation without compromising security. Leadership involvement is critical in reinforcing expectations and modeling responsible behavior.

By combining policy, technology, and culture, organizations can harness the benefits of AI while minimizing the risks associated with Shadow AI.

### **The Crawl, Walk, Run Model for AI Maturity**

An alternative approach to AI adoption is the  crawl, walk, run   framework**.**

While the AI maturity cycle outlines   *what*   organizations need to build, the  **crawl, walk, run**  model provides a practical lens for   *how quickly*   and   *at what level of complexity*   those capabilities should be implemented.

The crawl, walk, run model focuses   on starting small with pilot projects before expanding supervised AI capabilities and ultimately scaling up to more complex and sophisticated initiatives that may involve, for instance, the implementation of fully autonomous agentic AI systems (Silver, 2025).   The model enables organizations to build their AI capabilities gradually, manage risks, and generate value at each stage. (Ripla, 2025).

**Crawl**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303931/preview)

During the crawl phase, teams experiment with small-scale pilot programs and the automation of routine processes such as data entry, invoice processing, and report generation. Simultaneously, they invest in data hygiene by cleaning and centralizing information in secure repositories, and they conduct executive briefings and workshops to align stakeholders around AI principles and risks. Preliminary governance policies are formulated, though they may not yet be implemented at all levels of the organization.

**Walk**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303929/preview)

In the walk phase, organizations expand supervised AI capabilities. They introduce human‑in‑the‑loop systems that generate recommendations for tasks such as next‑best actions in sales or marketing, retaining human oversight to manage risk. AI‑powered chatbots or virtual assistants may be deployed in customer service functions to enhance response quality and gather user feedback. At this stage, data pipelines and analytics platforms are enhanced to support scaled AI workflows, and cross‑functional collaboration between business and technical teams facilitates knowledge sharing.

**Run**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303943/preview)

In the run phase, organizations enable fully agentic systems that operate autonomously within enterprise environments. Autonomous monitoring tools identify anomalies and initiate corrective actions in IT and security operations. Advanced recommendation engines deliver personalized experiences at scale for marketing, product development, and service delivery. To sustain these capabilities, a Center of Excellence may oversee AI governance, model monitoring, and the dissemination of best practices. Continuous evaluation of return on investment (ROI) through defined key performance indicators (KPIs) ensures ongoing refinement of both agent behaviors and governance protocols.

### **Supercharging with Agentic AI**

Although we might find the previous set of examples staggering in their speed, scope, and efficiency, let us look at how that process can be taken a step further with agentic AI.

Agentic AI takes generative capabilities and adds the capacity to operate across time, act independently, pursue goals, and manage sequences of tasks. Rather than being reactive, these systems are proactive and adaptive.

While many of these benefits are becoming evident, it helps to name them clearly. Agentic AI brings strategic advantages.

* **Speed**  : Product development cycles shrink dramatically, allowing for rapid iteration.
* **Cost-Efficiency**  : Labor-intensive tasks like QA, documentation, or design review can be handled by agents.
* **Scalability**  : Organizations can manage multiple product lines or experiments in parallel.
* **Quality and Consistency**  : With standardized logic and continual monitoring, agentic systems enforce best practices without burnout or distraction.

### **Getting Ready**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7304006/preview)

So how do we get started on this exciting journey of AI-enabled design? The transition to agentic AI does not require reinventing everything, but it does require intentional shifts in several areas.

* **People**  : Train designers, developers, and managers to work  *with*  AI agents, including how to prompt, supervise, and trust them.
* **Tools**  : Invest in platforms that allow for agent orchestration (e.g., frameworks that connect LLMs to APIs, data sources, and task systems).
* **Processes**  : Modify agile workflows to incorporate agent contributions (e.g., AI as part of daily stand-ups or release planning).
* **Mindset**  : Move from command-and-control leadership to collaborative supervision. Think of AI not as replacing humans, but as augmenting what teams can take on.

Taken together, these shifts suggest a future where product development is faster, more creative, and more adaptive, not because AI takes over the work, but because it enables people to do more of the  *right*  work, with better results.

### **Risks and Limitations**

AI-enabled browsers open exciting possibilities, but they also introduce serious risks that leaders must manage carefully. Data leaks, misinformation, and overreliance can all undermine trust, damage reputations, and expose organizations to legal or regulatory consequences. These challenges remind us that while the technology is powerful, it is not foolproof. To use AI responsibly, businesses must pair adoption with strong guardrails, training, and oversight.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303759/preview)

**Data Leakage**

The most immediate risk is employees pasting sensitive code or documents into consumer AI tools from within the browser, unintentionally exposing trade secrets or personal data. In 2023, Samsung temporarily banned generative-AI tools after staff uploaded confidential code, citing the possibility of external retention and reuse of that data (Park, 2023). Other firms moved similarly: Apple reportedly restricted staff use of external AI tools over confidentiality concerns (Vincent, 2023), and JPMorgan barred employee access to ChatGPT pending policy and privacy reviews (Cerullo, 2023). The lesson is simple: treat browser-based AI as a potential outbound data channel unless guardrails are in place.

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303784/preview)

**Misinformation**

Another major limitation of AI-enabled browsers is the risk of inaccurate or biased summaries. Large language models are known to generate content that “sounds right” but is factually wrong, a phenomenon often described as hallucination. In early 2024, Microsoft was threatened with fines of over 2 billion (USD) by the EU because of misinformation generated by their search engine, underscoring the challenges of accuracy at scale (Coingeek, 2024). When applied to financial filings, medical research, or policy documents, even small inaccuracies can lead to poor decisions, physical harm, reputational harm, or regulatory breaches.

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303758/preview)

**Overreliance**

Convenience can tempt employees to accept AI-generated summaries or suggestions in the browser without verifying them. That is risky because large language models are prone to confident errors. The now famous example of the lawyer, Steven Schwart, who used Chat GPT to prepare his case against Colombia-based Avianca Airlines, should be a lesson to us all. Unbeknownst to Schwarts, his “research” included, “bogus judicial decisions with bogus quotes and bogus internal citations.” In short, it was fiction: convincing sounding gibberish.

More recent misfires demonstrate that we are still in the era of hallucinations: Google’s AI Overviews has answered basic prompts incorrectly, including asserting the wrong year (Wired, 2025).

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303792/preview)

### **Conclusion**

Browsers are no longer neutral windows onto the web; they are becoming AI-enabled workspaces that influence how employees access information, make decisions, and collaborate. This transformation elevates the browser from background utility to a strategic enabler of productivity and influence across the enterprise.

For leaders, the opportunity lies in recognizing this shift and guiding adoption. Those who train employees to use browser-based AI effectively will see faster workflows, sharper insights, and stronger cross-functional collaboration. However, the risks, such as data leakage, misinformation, overreliance, and shadow AI, make it clear that leadership cannot simply let adoption spread informally. Analysts already warn that ungoverned AI use creates compliance and reputational risks for firms.

The practical path forward mirrors earlier digital transitions: start with clear policies, pilot tools with selected teams, establish IT-approved extensions and AI assistants, and then scale with training and governance frameworks. Leaders who take this approach will capture the efficiency gains of AI browsers while safeguarding compliance and trust.

# 6. Agent Architecture in the Enterprise: Centralized vs. Embedded

*Original URL: https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/pages/6-agent-architecture-in-the-enterprise-centralized-vs-embedded?module_item_id=3531875*

## **6. Agent Architecture in the Enterprise: Centralized vs. Embedded**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7304020/preview)

### **The Issue of Central and Embedded**

Although   the title of this unit may sound highly technical   and   abstract, the   central ideas are common to other realms.   Imagine a global clothing brand known for high‑quality, handmade garments.   Let’s   consider two   possible versions   of this store.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

**Version 1**

In one version of the business, every store around the world orders from a central catalog. Whether a customer visits the shop in New York or Nairobi, they see the same scarves and jackets. The experience is consistent, the supply chain is optimized, and inventory is easier to manage.

**Version 2**

Now imagine another version of the same brand, but structured more like a franchise. Each store can purchase items locally, reflect regional tastes, and even feature products made by artisans in the community. There is more variation across stores, but the offerings feel more tailored to the climate, traditions, and aesthetic preferences of each region.

As you might imagine, neither model is right or superior, but each will produce very different results. These two versions of the store map neatly onto the choices organizations face when designing their internal agent architecture:

* a   **centralized system**, where a single general‑purpose assistant supports the enterprise from a core location
* an   **embedded model**, where specialized agents live inside departmental tools and workflows.

The decisions you make at this stage will specifically influence performance, governance, and ROI with each approach carrying distinct advantages and limitations. Understanding the trade‑offs is critical for any organization seeking to scale AI agents from pilot experiments to business‑critical infrastructure.

In the following section, we will guide you through a high-level review focusing on the differences between the two systems, what this means for employees, as well as how it affects cost, security, and governance.

(Martyr, 2025)

### **The Centralized Agent Model**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303755/preview)

In a centralized model, an organization typically deploys one or two powerful agents that serve as general‑purpose assistants across departments. These agents often operate through familiar platforms like Slack or Microsoft Teams and are supported by an orchestration layer that routes user queries to the right systems.

Centralized agents are most useful when an organization is just beginning to explore the use of generative or agentic AI. They create a common interface for employees, simplify governance, and help leadership understand where demand and friction points exist across different departments.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303860/preview)

**Example: Valeria and AskNova**

Valeria works in operations at a mid-sized logistics company. Every morning, she opens Slack and starts her day with a few quick questions to the company’s agent, AskNova. She asks, “How many PTO days do I have left?” and gets an instant reply based on her HR record. Later, she requests a summary of a 27-page vendor contract. AskNova extracts the key terms, flags unusual clauses, and provides a plain-language summary she can forward to her manager.

Valeria never leaves Slack. She does not need to log into HR software, open a legal folder, or ping someone in legal operations. Behind the scenes, AskNova uses a single LLM-based orchestration layer that queries various systems through APIs. Valeria does not see the complexity, but she benefits from it.

This centralized setup creates a smooth, consistent user experience and significantly reduces first-line requests to HR, IT, and legal departments. It also allows the AI team to monitor queries and improve the assistant over time using one shared pipeline.

### **The Embedded Agent Model**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303750/preview)

As organizations mature in their use of AI, some discover the limits of centralization. While general‑purpose agents can handle many types of requests, they often fall short in specialized domains. Departments such as finance, marketing, and design may need agents trained on unique data, tools, and workflows.

In the embedded model, smaller agents are placed directly inside the systems teams already use. A finance agent might live in Excel or a planning platform. A design assistant might operate within Figma or Notion. These agents are context‑aware, tailored to the specific needs of the function, and can execute more complex or high‑stakes tasks.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303864/preview)

**Example: The Marketing Team Evolves**

When the centralized assistant was first deployed at a consumer goods company, it proved useful for answering HR questions and summarizing meeting transcripts. But the marketing team quickly reached its limits with it. Their work required creative ideation, brand nuance, and campaign-specific knowledge. The centralized agent offered bland suggestions, ignored the company’s voice, and occasionally reused outdated slogans.

Eventually, the company spun up a specialized marketing agent trained on historical campaigns, brand guidelines, and customer personas. It was embedded into their project management tool, where it could generate drafts, compare tone variants, and even suggest social copy based on recent product launches. Unlike the general agent, this embedded assistant understood context and offered content ready for immediate use.

This shift marked the beginning of a more federated, embedded approach across departments.

### **Key Differences Between Centralized and Embedded Architectures**

Below, we offer a comprehensive table summarizing the differences between the two systems.

| **Category** | **Centralized Agent** | **Embedded Agent** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Deployment Location** | Communication platform (Slack, Teams) | Departmental tools (e.g., Figma, Excel) |
| **Scope** | Broad and general-purpose | Narrow and domain-specific |
| **Ownership** | Typically, IT or automation teams | Owned by functional departments |
| **Training Data** | General enterprise knowledge | Function-specific datasets and workflows |
| **Governance** | Easier to control and audit | Harder to standardize across instances |
| **Adaptability** | Slower to adapt to specialized tasks | Highly tuned to local needs |
| **Speed of Deployment** | Faster to launch across company | Requires more setup per department |
| **Cost** | Lower initial cost | Higher ongoing maintenance cost |

### **Security, Compliance, and Governance**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303746/preview)

Security and compliance are also important considerations when choosing an agent architecture.  Centralized agents  are generally easier to manage because controls such as access permissions, logging, and audit trails can be handled in one place. This makes it simpler to meet standards such as HIPAA and other data protection regulations.

Embedded agents, on the other hand, often operate across different tools, data sets, and teams. Because they are distributed, they can introduce risks if not managed carefully—for example, through inconsistent permissions, data exposure, or gaps in oversight.

To address this, many organizations adopt a hybrid approach with shared governance. This allows teams to build specialized solutions while maintaining consistent standards for security, compliance, and accountability across the enterprise (Satish, 2025).

*Select each tab to learn more.*

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303866/preview)

**U.S. Example: HIPAA in a Decentralized Healthcare Organization**

Consider a U.S.‑based health care network exploring agent deployment across departments. A centralized agent that handles general scheduling queries or policy FAQs can be contained within strict access controls and monitored for compliance with HIPAA requirements. But when a specialized agent is embedded directly into a patient intake system or billing platform, the risk exposure increases. If the embedded agent generates, stores, or even temporarily processes protected health information (PHI) without proper safeguards—such as encryption, access logs, and business associate agreements—the organization could face regulatory penalties or legal liability. In this case, centralizing sensitive data access through a governed layer may not only improve efficiency, but also reduce compliance risk.

### **A Hybrid Path: Centralized Core with Embedded Specialists**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303751/preview)

Most organizations do not stay in one model forever. Instead, they evolve toward a hybrid approach. A centralized agent remains in place as a gateway or “generalist,” handling common tasks and routing more complex queries to embedded specialists. Departments that reach a certain level of maturity or scale develop their own agents tailored to their needs.

This mirrors the clothing brand analogy. The core product line continues to come from central sourcing, ensuring consistency and brand recognition. But regional shops gain permission to offer locally sourced garments that reflect the needs of their customers. The result is a flexible architecture that combines the strengths of both models.

Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Meaning/Description |
| --- | --- |
| Agent Architecture | The structural design and organizational framework that defines how AI agents are built, deployed, and interact within an enterprise. |
| Agentic AI | AI systems that can perceive, reason, make decisions, and take actions autonomously with minimal human intervention. |
| AI Orchestrator | A multi-agent system that coordinates many specialized AI agents. For instance, a clinical orchestrator might trigger one agent to review labs, another to analyze imaging, and then combine their outputs into a unified recommendation. |
| Centralized Agents | AI agents deployed as a shared, general-purpose system across an organization, typically accessed through a single interface and governed through unified controls for consistency, security, and oversight. |
| Clinical Decision Support (CDS) | Software tools that help clinicians make diagnoses or treatment decisions by analyzing patient data (e.g. symptoms, labs, images) and offering recommendations. Agentic CDS can actively gather more data and refine its advice. |
| Data Loss Prevention (DLP) | Security tools and strategies aimed at preventing sensitive data from being accessed, shared, or leaked outside authorized channels. |
| Electronic Health Record (EHR) | A digital system for storing patient health information (histories, notes, test results). Agentic AI often integrates with EHRs to access and update patient data. |
| Embedded Agents | AI agents integrated directly within specific tools, platforms, or departmental workflows, designed with specialized knowledge and context to perform targeted, function-specific tasks. |
| Interoperability (e.g. HL7, FHIR) | Standards and protocols that allow different healthcare IT systems (EHRs, lab systems, imaging centers) to share data. Agents rely on interoperability to gather all needed patient data seamlessly. |
| KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) | KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) are measurable values that indicate how effectively an individual, team, or organization is achieving specific business objectives. |
| LLM (Large Language Model) | Large Language Models (LLMs) are advanced artificial intelligence systems trained on massive amounts of text data to understand, generate, and manipulate human language. They use deep learning techniques to perform tasks like answering questions, writing content, summarizing information, etc. |
| Multi-Agent System | A system of several independent AI agents working together or in parallel. In healthcare, one agent might handle scheduling while another analyzes data, all coordinated by an orchestrator agent. |
| Orchestration | The coordination and management of multiple AI agents and processes to achieve business objectives. |
| Personalized (Precision) Medicine | Tailoring medical treatment to individual patient characteristics (genetics, biomarkers). Agentic AI enables this by analyzing complex data to suggest individualized therapies. |
| Product Development Lifecycle (PDLC) | The process necessary to create a new product, going from ideation and prototyping to launch and post-launch iteration. |
| RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) | A technique that combines AI generation with information retrieval to provide more accurate and contextual responses. |
