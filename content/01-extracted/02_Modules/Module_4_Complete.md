## **Welcome to Module 4: Cybersecurity: Classic Scenarios, Agent Risks, Disinformation, and Systemic Impact**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303657/preview)

As AI systems grow more autonomous and embedded across industries, the cybersecurity landscape is shifting in both obvious and invisible ways. Threats like misinformation, impersonation, and data exfiltration are no longer confined to human hackers; they are increasingly generated, executed, or accelerated by intelligent systems themselves. 

This module traces the evolution of those risks, starting with the “classic” era of cybersecurity, where breaches often began with a spoofed email or a single click on a malicious file. Through realistic case studies that shed light on the inner workings of hacking, we will explore how even well-meaning users can trigger high-impact failures during moments of digital transformation. 

But as we move into the AI-infused present, the threat vectors become harder to detect and predict. Learning these new skills of detection and prediction can make the difference between safety and sabotage. Deepfakes, AI-generated impersonation, and prompt injection attacks do not just target systems; they manipulate perception and erode trust in what is real. These tactics can bypass traditional defenses and operate in real time, creating unprecedented security challenges. 

Equally urgent, though less visible, are the internal risks posed by AI itself. Hallucinated outputs, embedded bias, reasoning failures, and opaque decision-making can all result in reputational, financial, or physical harm, even without malicious intent. In high-stakes environments like healthcare, finance, or public policy, these failures are not just technical bugs; they are strategic liabilities. 

By the end of this module, you will understand the full spectrum of threats: from the external to the internal, from the intentional to the emergent. And more importantly, you will be equipped to act, through frameworks, foresight, and readiness to lead securely in an AI-powered world.

### **The Classic Era of Cybersecurity Risks**

Before we explore the new risks introduced by generative and agentic AI, it is worth reviewing the kinds of cybersecurity challenges that emerged during earlier waves of digital transformation.

As organizations shifted from paper systems to cloud infrastructure and connected digital tools, they unlocked enormous gains in efficiency, scale, and speed. But those same qualities made them more vulnerable to attack. One compromised email or misjudged click could expose an entire organization.  These attacks were largely human-triggered and rule-based.

To illustrate this, let’s look at a cautionary tale: a single employee, a convincing message, and a costly breach.

After this example, we will return to the present, where the attack vectors are no longer just documents and links, but synthetic media, autonomous agents, and intelligent deception.

### **Case Study: Lisa Mendez**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303806/preview)

**The Victim**

Lisa Mendez is the HR manager at Midpoint Financial, a mid-sized investment firm undergoing digital transformation. One of her key responsibilities involves evaluating new HR platforms and modernizing internal workflows.

**The Context**

Recently, she attended a virtual HR tech webinar and was thrilled to meet peers from other companies, who were also navigating the excitement and complexity of digital transformation. She felt energized, encouraged, and validated—convinced that exchanging contact information with her new network could sharpen her thinking and accelerate progress in digitizing Midpoint’s HR systems.

**The Set-up**

So, when she received a friendly follow-up email from someone who claimed to be an attendee, thanking her for the conversation and including an “HR Connect, Final Attendee List.xlsx,” Lisa saw no red flags. The tone was warm, the branding looked familiar, and the context made sense.

**The Bait**

Curious to see if her contacts were listed, Lisa opened the Excel file and, following the prompt in the email, enabled  macros  to "view the full sheet." Unbeknownst to her, this action installed a   remote access trojan (RAT)  on her system.

**The Attack**

Over the next week, attackers used Lisa’s compromised credentials to silently navigate Midpoint’s internal systems. They located a shared folder containing archived onboarding documents, including scanned IDs and bank account info for several high-net-worth clients.

The attackers then used Lisa’s internal access to generate a trusted service request, tricking the finance department into initiating several “urgent” wire transfers.

**The Inadequate Response**

By the time Midpoint’s cybersecurity team flagged an unusual login from an offshore IP address, over    $4.3 million had been siphoned from investor accounts. News of the breach spread quickly, triggering a client exodus and regulatory scrutiny.

**The End of the Story**

Lisa, devastated and confused, would never have suspected that she had been the entry point.

### **The Current Era of Cybersecurity Risks**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303805/preview)

Lisa’s story represents a typical case from the world of classical digital transformation: as organizations moved from paper to cloud, the scale and speed of cyberattacks grew exponentially. Systems were connected, data was centralized, and attackers took advantage.

But now we face something new. As we move from digitization to AI-injected digital transformation, new risks emerge and many of them are harder to detect, anticipate, or explain.  The shift is from user error to system-level deception and autonomy-driven risks.  Autonomy, generation, impersonation, and agent decision-making are reshaping the threat landscape.

So, what does Lisa need to learn next? How can she, and how can you, prepare not just for phishing emails, but for AI-driven deception, misinformation, and agent failure? That is where we go next.

### **Deepfakes**

Deepfakes are synthetic media, usually video or audio, that use AI to convincingly mimic real people’s appearance or voice. They are created using powerful machine learning models such as GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks) or diffusion-based methods that we studied previously, and they are improving rapidly in believability and accessibility. (Northwestern, 2023)   
  
  High-profile examples include fabricated videos of Elon Musk   and   U.S. President Joe Biden.

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303804/preview)

See [Deepfake crypto scam uses Elon Musk to target investors](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dRgUk2jEmeM).

In January 2025, AARP reported on a wave of scam advertisements and deepfake videos using Elon Musk’s image and likeness to promote fake cryptocurrency investments and fraudulent products. In one case, a retiree lost more than $300,000 after being persuaded to invest through a platform falsely presented as connected to Musk and his team. The case illustrates how AI-generated deepfakes can exploit public trust in high-profile business figures to influence financial decisions and steal victims’ savings (Ianzito, 2025).

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303802/preview)

Listen to [Fake Biden robocall urges Democrats to skip New Hampshire primary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FCs_zFbkf0M).

One of the most high-profile and politically disturbing uses of deepfakes came in January 2024, just before the New Hampshire primary. Thousands of voters received a robocall featuring an AI-generated voice clone of President Joe Biden, urging them   *not*   to vote in the primary, falsely suggesting their vote would count more in November.   (Bond, 2024)

While these are political examples, they underscore the power of technology and the urgency of being able to detect and respond to the threat of synthetic media.

In a business context, the risks are equally serious. Imagine a deepfake video of your organization’s CEO announcing a mass layoff or merger. If released on social media or sent directly to stakeholders, it could destabilize investor confidence, trigger employee panic, or move markets before anyone has a chance to correct the narrative.

### **Impersonation**

Unlike deepfakes (broadcast), impersonation is targeted and interactive.   Impersonation in AI contexts refers to the use of generative tools, typically voice cloning, chatbots, or scripted agents, to convincingly pose as a trusted individual or brand. While this tactic predates AI (e.g., phishing emails), modern tools make impersonation faster, more scalable, and far more convincing.

Unlike deepfakes, which are typically aimed at large public audiences through video or social media, impersonation often targets individuals or small groups in private channels, making it more interactive, personalized, and difficult to detect in the moment.   
(Sjouwerman, 2024)

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303803/preview)

A notable example occurred in the energy sector in 2019, when cybercriminals used an AI-generated voice clone of a German CEO to call a British executive and request a fraudulent transfer of $243,000. The employee complied, believing the request was genuine, highlighting how persuasive audio impersonation can bypass traditional defenses. (Damiani, 2019)

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303801/preview)

In early 2024, a finance team member in Hong Kong received a phishing email and joined a video conference with senior executives, only to find the participants were deepfake-generated clones of true leaders.

Convinced of the call’s authenticity, the employee authorized 15 transfers totaling approximately HK$200 million (≈ US $25.6 million) before the breach was uncovered. The case illustrates how realistic impersonation can bypass human judgment and significant internal controls.   
(Milmo, 2024)

For businesses, the implications are serious. Executive spoofing scams can lead to major financial losses. Chatbots, if poorly secured, can also be manipulated to impersonate internal systems or personnel, misleading employees or leaking sensitive information.

Unlike deepfakes, impersonation is generally harder to defend as “dual-use,” though some voice cloning applications (e.g., for personalized customer service or accessibility tools) show potential in controlled contexts. We will revisit those uses in a later portion of this course when we explore positive use cases of generative AI.

### **Prompt Injection**

Prompt injection is a cybersecurity exploit where attackers insert malicious or hidden instructions into the input (prompt) given to an AI system, tricking it into behaving in unintended ways.   Simply put, this is   like inserting hidden instructions into a conversation that the AI mistakenly follows. This can result in dangerous behaviors,   such as bypassing safeguards or revealing sensitive information.   It attacks the AI’s decision-making logic using language rather than code.

(Kosinski, 2024)

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303800/preview)

One well-documented case occurred in February 2023, when a Stanford student used prompt injection against Microsoft’s Bing chatbot (codenamed Sydney), and got the system to divulge its programming.   
  
(Kosinkski, 2024)

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7304017/preview)

Another example, reported by researchers from UC San Diego, involved a vulnerability called “Imprompter,” where hidden instructions embedded in text triggered chatbots to leak personal chat logs to a hacker-controlled domain with an ~80% success rate.   
  
(Wired, 2024)

In a business setting, prompt injection could trick an AI assistant into sending confidential documents to outsiders or generate misleading analytics, which could damage reputation, privacy, and decision integrity.

While prompt injection is a serious risk, it also highlights the need for strong prompt hygiene, sandboxing of agent workflows, and layered human oversight, topics we will cover later in this course.

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303846/preview)

### **Response and Prevention**

Having looked at the classic and current threats we face, we now turn to response and prevention. In doing so, we will explore frameworks that help break security down into manageable and actionable steps.

The first framework is the Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain.   The framework helps guard against human-triggered and AI-driven threats by breaking a typical attack down into seven distinct phases, from early reconnaissance to final data exfiltration. Each step offers an opportunity to detect, delay, or stop the attack before serious damage is done.

### **Response**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7304051/preview)

Although originally focused on threats like malware or phishing, the Kill Chain has gained relevance in an AI-infused digital landscape. Today, it can help organizations respond to new risks such as prompt injection, AI-assisted impersonation, or deepfake-based social engineering. Understanding this model allows leaders to visualize the anatomy of a modern attack and to craft smarter, layered defenses.   
  
(Sheps, 2024)

### **Prevention**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303843/preview)

The only thing better than a swift, intelligent response is timely, methodical prevention—the cybersecurity equivalent of taking your vitamins daily. Prevention may lack the drama of crisis response, but it is where real resilience is built.

As we shift focus to proactive defense, we will once again turn to a framework—this time from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), a U.S. agency that develops widely adopted standards for technology, security, and innovation across industries.

Their Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) is a flexible, practical model designed to help organizations of all sizes manage and reduce cybersecurity risk. Rather than prescribing specific tools or vendors, the CSF breaks cybersecurity down into five core categories that guide teams in identifying threats, protecting assets, detecting anomalies, responding effectively, and recovering efficiently.   
  
(IBM, 2021)

What makes this framework especially valuable for leaders—not just IT teams—is its emphasis on actionable, strategic alignment between cybersecurity and organizational goals.

In the table that follows, we will walk through each of the five categories in plain language, along with practical, low-barrier actions that your organization can take to align with this approach.

### **The Power of Perception in AI Agents**

Just as our senses gather information about the world and our brain processes it to understand our surroundings, AI also processes data from various sources, analyzes it, and acts on it. However, for AI, perception is not as straightforward as it is for humans. Unlike a person who can directly see or hear, AI systems require hardware like cameras, microphones, and other sensors.

The significance of AI  **perception**  powers cannot be understated: They are the gateway through which AI agents make sense of their ecosystem, fueling every decision and action they take.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

**Seeing the big picture** 

AI agents rely on perceptual inputs to build an internal map of their surroundings, identifying objects, people, and events in context. Whether navigating a crowded warehouse or scanning a dashboard of metrics, accurate perception is the first step toward meaningful interaction.

 **Spotting patterns and anomalies** 

Once sensory data arrives, AI agents deploy pattern‑recognition algorithms to find recurring structures such as faces in a crowd, spikes in network traffic, or shifts in customer sentiment. Perceptual acuity also helps detect outliers, helping to flag potential risks or new opportunities.

 **Adapting on the fly** 

Perception is a continuous feedback loop. As environments change, agents reassess their perceptions, recalibrate models and pivot their behavior, ensuring resilience in dynamic, unpredictable settings.

 **Sensing to source data** 

From high‑resolution cameras to microphones and force sensors, perception channels raw data into an agent’s decision engine. These sensory streams form the bedrock of analysis, turning the noise of real‑world signals into actionable insights.

 **Grounding decisions in reality** 

Informed by what they perceive, agents can weigh alternatives and choose the option which is most likely to succeed. A delivery drone, for example, adjusts its route based on live weather feeds; a virtual assistant tailors its suggestions by interpreting your tone of voice.

 **Engaging seamlessly with people** 

Effective user interaction hinges on reliable perception. Speech‑ and gesture‑recognition allow agents to “listen” and “respond” naturally, while sentiment analysis gauges user satisfaction, delivering feedback, guidance, or support in a way that feels intuitive.

(Stryker, n.d.)

In an age where AI agents increasingly partner with humans and systems alike,   perception   is   not just a technical   detail;   it   is   the very lens through which these agents interpret reality,   make   judgments,   and drive value for your organization.

### **Hallucinations, Inaccuracies, and Misinformation**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7304053/preview)

Hallucinations occur when a model generates information that is ungrounded in its training data or the prompt (Wei et al., 2022). This occurs because LLMs are not intrinsically capable of checking their own work; rather, they generate responses based on probabilities rather than verified truths (Dayal, 2025).

Hallucinations can contribute to a wave of misinformation as AI-generated fabrications spread online, which, as we have already seen in this module, can have negative consequences for individuals, companies, and countries. For organizations, the spread of AI-generated inaccuracies or misinformation can lead to issues such as financial losses, eroded trust, and damaged reputation, especially in high-stakes regulated industries like finance and health care.

Although we will explore some real-world cases later, for now, let’s try to visualize how an AI hallucination may dramatically affect medication dosing for patients with chronic illnesses using the fictionalized scenario below.

### **Bias and Fairness**

LLMs learn from vast data sets, which can inadvertently contain and replicate harmful societal biases. The models may be capable of working at incredible speeds to generate text, images, and even solutions to complex customer problems, yet they lack “true understanding, moral reasoning, and ethical judgment” (Dayal, 2025).

Without efforts to mitigate these biases through human oversight and governance, AI systems may amplify biases, leading to unfair or discriminatory outcomes in high stakes applications such as hiring, lending, or law enforcement, and causing reputational harm to the parties who used the AI systems (Streets, 2024; Dayal, 2025).

### **Safeguards**

Having explored how AI systems can misfire—through bias, opacity, or unintended outputs—it is essential to implement safeguards   before deployment. Safeguards are proactive measures built into AI systems during design and development to reduce risk, improve fairness, and ensure alignment with organizational and ethical standards. By strengthening the system at its foundation, organizations can prevent many issues from arising in the first place.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

**Data curation and debiasing** 

Careful selection, filtering, and annotation of training data are essential to reducing bias. Techniques such as counterfactual data augmentation and adversarial testing can help identify and correct imbalances before models are deployed.

 **Model guardrails and access controls** 

Technical safeguards such as content filters, usage restrictions, and role-based access controls help prevent misuse. These mechanisms limit harmful outputs, restrict sensitive actions, and ensure that only authorized users can interact with critical system functions.

 **Diverse and representative training data** 

Training AI systems on diverse data sets helps prevent the amplification of existing biases. Ensuring representation across demographics, geographies, and contexts improves both fairness and real-world applicability.

 **Value alignment** 

AI systems should be designed to reflect human values and organizational goals. This includes aligning outputs with ethical guidelines, regulatory requirements, and intended use cases to minimize harmful or unintended behavior.

(Streets, 2024;   Anisuzzaman   et al., 2025; Dayal,   2025)

### **Agentic AI Errors**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303849/preview)

In addition to the challenges posed by LLMs in general, there are some issues specifically related to agentic systems. Autonomous AI agents make decisions and execute actions with minimal oversight, introducing a margin of error that is no longer human-controlled. Small miscalculations can escalate rapidly, potentially leading to significant financial losses.

The     “black box”     nature of LLMs, where their internal logic is difficult to explain, complicates accountability and legal responsibility when something goes wrong. This autonomy can lead to unpredictable behavior, task-drift, and errors, especially as workflows become more complex. (Sanchez, 2025)

### **Mitigation Strategies for Errors in Agentic AI Systems**

Even with strong safeguards in place, AI systems are not immune to error. Issues such as hallucinations, bias, and flawed reasoning can still emerge once systems are deployed and interacting with real-world data, users, and environments. This is especially true for agentic systems, where autonomous decision making introduces additional risk and small errors can escalate rapidly.

Mitigation strategies focus on monitoring, intervention, and continuous improvement after deployment. These approaches enable organizations to detect unreliable outputs, escalate complex situations to human experts, and refine system performance over time. In practice, mitigation is what ensures that AI systems remain trustworthy, adaptable, and aligned with organizational goals in dynamic, real-world conditions.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

**Human oversight and fact-checking**

Due to LLMs' lack of intrinsic fact-checking abilities, human oversight is critical for monitoring outputs, flagging inaccuracies, and ensuring alignment with ethical guidelines. (Dayal, 2025).

**Seamless handoff to human agents**

For complex or nuanced customer inquiries that require deeper understanding or judgment, businesses must ensure that there is a seamless handoff between AI agents and humans. (Uzoka et al., 2024)

**Continuous training and updates**

Regular updates and continuous training are necessary to maintain and improve the accuracy and reliability of AI output. (Uzoka et al., 2024)

**Reasoning and confidence scores**

Inducing models to explain their reasoning or providing a confidence score can help to address hallucinations. (Anisuzzaman et al., 2025)

**Media literacy initiatives**

Educating users to understand and protect themselves from synthetic misinformation and disinformation is crucial (The Future of Synthetic Media, 2024)

**Iterative refinement and feedback loops**

Continuously refining outputs based on user interactions and feedback is essential and over time, will help organizations generate more value from their AI initiatives. (Deloitte, 2025)

# 4. Blockchain for AI Security and Trust

## **4. Blockchain for AI Security and Trust**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303848/preview)

A growing challenge for enterprise leaders is figuring out how to trust AI agents—especially when those systems are making high-stakes decisions with real financial or operational impact. Many of these decisions happen with limited transparency, making it difficult to understand how outcomes are reached or to verify whether systems meet regulatory expectations. Traditional security approaches, which were designed for more predictable software, often fall short in this new, more autonomous environment.

This is where blockchain begins to play a new role. While it is best known for powering cryptocurrencies, its real value in this context is as a trust layer—one that can create transparent, tamperproof records of AI activity. Rather than focusing only on using AI to analyze blockchain data, organizations are increasingly exploring the reverse: using blockchain to make AI systems themselves more secure, accountable, and trustworthy.

### **Immutable AI Governance Through Blockchain**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7304052/preview)

Establishing immutable governance mechanisms is essential for maintaining transparency and trust in autonomous AI systems. Blockchain technology enables this by securely recording the entire life cycle and identity of AI models and agents, ensuring their integrity and accountability. This foundational trust framework paves the way for more detailed mechanisms such as immutable model provenance and decentralized identity management, which are critical components in robust AI governance.

*Select each tab to learn more.*

**Immutable model provenance**

* Blockchain creates a tamperproof ledger capturing the full life cycle of AI models, including model versions, training data fingerprints, performance metrics, and deployment conditions (Rapid Innovation, 2024).
* This immutable record ensures a complete audit trail, prevents unauthorized changes, facilitates regulatory compliance, and enables rapid rollback if needed.
* By automating model governance, organizations increase transparency, reduce errors, and build stakeholder trust.

**Decentralized identity for AI agents**

* Traditional centralized authentication is insufficient for autonomous AI agents interacting across organizations.
* Blockchain-based decentralized identity frameworks assign unique cryptographic identities to agents, enabling self-sovereignty and cross-platform interoperability.
* Key components include decentralized identifiers (DIDs), verifiable credentials, secure communication protocols, and reputation systems to maintain agent trustworthiness (IBM, 2025).
* This approach enhances secure collaboration across distributed networks without requiring complex integration agreements.

### **Example**

**JPMorgan Chase’s Blockchain—AI Trading Platform**

JPMorgan Chase shows how blockchain and AI can be combined to solve real enterprise challenges while delivering measurable value. To ensure trust and compliance in AI-driven trading, they developed a blockchain-based governance system that shifts risk management from reactive to proactive.

Built on a private Ethereum blockchain, the system provides secure, controlled access. Trading models are registered using cryptographic hashes to prevent tampering, while performance data is stored immutably for accurate tracking and regulatory compliance. Smart contracts automate reporting, reducing manual errors and ensuring consistency.

A decentralized authentication system assigns each AI agent a unique blockchain identity. Smart contracts then control access to specific trading functions based on predefined rules and risk parameters. All agent decisions are recorded on the blockchain, creating a complete audit trail, while automated controls enforce trading limits and reduce operational risk.

Measurable business impact:

* **99.9%**  audit compliance with zero manual intervention
* **60%**  reduction in compliance reporting overhead
* **45%**  faster incident response
* **100%**  traceability of AI decisions and model changes
* **$15**  million in annual compliance savings

This approach addresses a core challenge of autonomous AI: maintaining oversight and compliance without sacrificing speed. By embedding governance directly into the infrastructure, JPMorgan created AI systems that become more trustworthy as they scale.

(Codora, 2025)

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303862/preview)

### **Advanced Smart Contract Governance**

AI-powered smart contracts take blockchain governance a step further by automating key compliance and decision-making processes. These contracts execute automatically when predefined conditions are met, reducing the need for manual oversight and lowering the risk of human error. The result is a more transparent, consistent, and reliable system.

As the technology evolves, smart contracts are becoming more dynamic. They can adapt to changing conditions, support AI-assisted dispute resolution, and even anticipate compliance risks before they occur. Instead of treating compliance as a reactive requirement, organizations can use these tools to manage it proactively—and even turn it into a source of efficiency and value.

#### **Key aspects of advanced smart contract governance:**

*Select each tab to learn more.*

**Automated compliance and risk management**

Smart contracts can autonomously enforce data usage policies, regulate transaction workflows, generate immutable audit trails, and promptly detect policy violations in real time, thereby reducing fraud risks and operational overhead (AIBC World, 2024).

**Dynamic and self-modifying contracts**

Contracts can adapt terms based on AI insights and evolving business conditions, support automated arbitration to resolve disputes efficiently, and modify themselves within governance boundaries to meet new regulatory requirements (Codora, 2025).

**Predictive compliance monitoring**

Leveraging AI capabilities, smart contracts can foresee potential compliance issues before they arise, enabling organizations to intervene proactively and maintain continuous regulatory adherence.

This comprehensive automation allows organizations to shift focus from routine compliance tasks to strategic decision-making, enhances trust among stakeholders, and mitigates legal and financial risks associated with governance failures.

### **Security and Performance Optimization**

![](https://dppcampus.mitpeonline.com/courses/17497/files/7303847/preview)

Implementing blockchain–AI integration at enterprise scale requires sophisticated security and performance considerations. Cryptographic security leverages hardware security modules for private key storage, multisignature schemes for critical operations, regular key rotation policies for long-term security, and comprehensive backup and recovery procedures that ensure business continuity.

Performance optimization addresses the inherent tension between blockchain’s consensus requirements and AI’s need for speed through Layer 2 scaling solutions for high transaction volumes, state channels for frequent agent interactions, sharding approaches for parallel transaction processing, and hybrid architectures that use off-chain computation with on-chain verification to balance performance and security requirements.

### **Did You Know?**

AI predicts outcomes and automates complex decision-making processes, leading to more efficient and accurate contract execution, while blockchain generates vast amounts of data that AI analyzes effectively, creating a symbiotic relationship that enhances both technologies’ capabilities, meaning your blockchain infrastructure becomes smarter while your AI systems become more trustworthy through their integration (QuickNode, 2024).

Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Meaning/Description |
| --- | --- |
| Confidence Score (AI) | A metric provided by some AI systems that indicates the likelihood that the model’s output is correct. This score can help assess reliability but is not always available. |
| Context Window (LLM) | The amount of information a language model can retain and consider when generating a response. A limited context window can result in the omission of relevant details in complex tasks. |
| Cyber Kill Chain | A cybersecurity framework that divides an attack into seven stages, from reconnaissance to data exfiltration, and provides opportunities for detection and response at each phase. |
| Deepfake | AI-generated audio or video content that convincingly mimics real individuals. Deepfakes support impersonation, disinformation, or manipulation and are often difficult to detect without specialized tools. |
| Exfiltration | The final phase in many cyberattacks in which the attacker extracts sensitive data from the compromised system, often resulting in financial or reputational harm. |
| Impersonation Attack | A cybersecurity tactic in which attackers use AI tools such as chatbots, voice clones, or fake personas to pose as trusted individuals or brands to manipulate targets or steal data. |
| Least-Privilege Access | A security principle that ensures AI agents or users are granted only the minimum permissions required to perform their tasks, reducing the risk of misuse or compromise. |
| Phased Deployment (AI) | A strategy for gradually implementing AI systems, starting with low-risk use cases and increasing autonomy over time. This method helps reduce risk and improve oversight. |
| Prompt Injection | An exploit in which attackers insert hidden or malicious instructions into prompts directed at AI systems, causing them to act in unintended or harmful ways. |
| Reconnaissance (Cybersecurity) | The initial stage of a cyberattack in which the attacker gathers information about the target organization, often using public sources, to identify vulnerabilities. |
| Remote Access Trojan (RAT) | Malware that enables an attacker to gain unauthorized control over a computer system, often without the user’s knowledge. |
| Sandboxing | A cybersecurity technique used to isolate systems or processes, including AI agents, to prevent access to critical resources or limit harm if compromised. |
| Spoofing | The act of disguising communication from an unknown or untrusted source as being from a known, trusted source. AI can increase the scale and believability of spoofing attempts. |
| Synthetic Disinformation | False or misleading information created and distributed using AI-generated content, such as deepfake videos or voice clones, with the intent to deceive or manipulate. |
| Weaponization (Cybersecurity) | A stage in the Cyber Kill Chain in which attackers create malicious payloads, such as infected documents or prompt-based exploits, to target a specific individual or system. |
