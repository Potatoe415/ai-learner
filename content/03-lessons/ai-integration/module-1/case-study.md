In 2024 the Swedish fintech Klarna put an AI customer service assistant, powered by OpenAI, in front of its customers. MIT facilitators use it as a reference benchmark for AI in customer service: big numbers on volume and speed, and a clear split between what the machine handles and what people keep.

## Numbers

| Value | Label |
|---|---|
| ≈ 700 | full-time agents’ worth of work in the first 30 days |
| 11 → 2 min | average time to resolve an inquiry (about 82% faster) |
| −25% | repeat inquiries, thanks to more accurate resolution |
| $40M | projected yearly improvement in operating profit |

## Timeline

### 2024 · Launch

Klarna deploys an OpenAI-powered assistant on its customer service channel.

### First 30 days

The assistant absorbs a workload equal to about 700 full-time agents.

### Measured results

Average resolution drops from 11 minutes with human agents to about 2. Repeat inquiries fall by a quarter, a sign that more problems are solved on first contact.

### The split

Support divides in two: the assistant takes high-frequency transactional questions; humans keep complex disputes and fraud escalations.

### The projection

The deployment is expected to add about $40 million a year to operating profit. That is a forecast, not a measured saving.

## Lessons

- The gains came from frequent, well-defined transactions. That is where to look first in your own workflow.
- Speed is not quality. Falling repeat inquiries is the signal that requests are really resolved.
- The workforce was not removed: the design kept humans on disputes and fraud. Plan the hand-off on day one.
- Read headline money carefully. A projected profit figure is a hypothesis to test, the same way your own first cost estimate is.
- Compare like with like: the 11-minute human baseline is what makes the 2-minute figure meaningful. Your estimate needs a baseline too.
